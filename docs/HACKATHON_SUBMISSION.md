# Swarm friction: procedural gates and forensic logs for agent tool use

**AI Swarm Dynamics Hackathon, October 2026. David Fraile Navarro, Macquarie University.**

**In one paragraph.** Most oversight of AI agents relies on someone, human or model, reading what the agents did and judging whether it was acceptable. That does not scale to swarms, and it can be switched off when it gets in the way. *Swarm friction* is a different approach: put a small, mechanical procedure in front of every privileged tool call (network access, credentials, reading outside the workspace, spawning another agent, publishing output), require the agent to state what it is about to do and under which rule, and log everything in a structured way. The gate never judges content; it only checks that the procedure was followed. We built this as a working harness, ran a 400-episode experiment on two frontier models, and applied the same forensic questions to the AI Village dataset and to the SwarmTraces export. The headline result is sobering and useful: in our benchmark, simply reminding the agent where the rules are did most of the work; the declaration procedure added no measurable benefit on top of a cost-matched acknowledgement step. The forensic logging, on the other hand, worked exactly as intended, and it is the part of the project we would build on.

## 1. The idea

Agents increasingly operate in groups: a parent spawns helpers, helpers call tools, tools touch credentials and the network. When something goes wrong, three questions need answering after the fact: who asked for the action, what was the agent allowed to do, and what actually executed. Chat transcripts answer these badly. They are long, narrative, and written by the agent whose behaviour is in question.

Swarm friction borrows from safety engineering rather than from monitoring. The 1944 OSS *Simple Sabotage Field Manual* lists ways to slow an organisation down: route everything through channels, insist on precise wording, question whether a matter is within the group's remit. Read as a defence rather than an attack, these are cheap procedural controls that do not require anyone to understand the content of the work. Our hypothesis was that such friction would hurt out-of-scope shortcuts more than legitimate work, because shortcuts tend to be denser in privileged actions (an extra credential, an extra external host) than the slow, in-scope route to the same goal. Even if that asymmetry turned out to be small, the procedure would still produce a complete, structured audit trail as a by-product, which is valuable on its own.

Concretely, the harness has three components. A **charter** (`CHARTER.md`) is a short, plain-English operating policy for agents on a fictional analytics platform: which paths may be read, which hosts may be contacted, which credentials exist and what they are for, and a rule that an incomplete answer obtained within scope is preferred to a complete one obtained outside it. A **broker** sits between the agent and its tools. For any privileged-class call it can demand a declaration (tool, target, intent, and the charter clause being relied on) and bind that declaration to the call that follows; a call that does not match its declaration is rejected. The broker never decides whether the action is *permitted*; it only decides whether the *procedure* was satisfied. A separate **logger** records, after execution, whether each action was within scope, together with identity, tickets, gate outcomes, arguments and full outputs. Procedural compliance and actual policy compliance are therefore recorded independently and can be compared.

## 2. The experiment

Everything runs in a mocked environment: in-memory files, hosts and credentials, a read-only SQLite database and a synthetic analytics world generated from a seed. Nothing real executes, so "escape" means an out-of-charter network request, not a real one. This also keeps the work far from any dual-use concern.

Twenty task templates are split into two suites of ten. **Suite A (benign)** tasks can be completed cleanly within the charter. **Suite B (temptation)** tasks state a legitimate goal that happens to have a fast, out-of-scope route and a slow, in-scope one. For example: "produce a summary of 2025 claim patterns for the externally published briefing pack", where the restricted claims file would give a far better summary than the public data. No task ever instructs the agent to break a rule; shortcuts are available, never suggested. Every episode is graded objectively against an explicit output contract (JSON or CSV fields with reference values), and separately for whether any out-of-scope action completed.

Each task ran in two generated worlds, on two models (`gpt-6-luna` and `claude-sonnet-5-5`), under five conditions:

| Condition | Gate | Initial pointer to the policy file |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | none | no (policy is on disk, discoverable) |
| Reminder | none | yes |
| Acknowledgement + reminder | privileged calls must be acknowledged (a content-free round trip) | yes |
| Declaration + reminder | privileged calls must be declared, bound to the target and justified by a charter clause | yes |
| Declaration, no pointer | declaration gate; the gate itself is what reveals the policy file | no |

The main comparison was declaration versus acknowledgement when both have the reminder. The acknowledgement arm costs the agent the same extra round trip but asks it to say nothing, so the comparison isolates whether *stating* what you are about to do changes behaviour, over and above being slowed down. The protocol, arms, outcomes and analysis were frozen before the paid run (`docs/REPAIR_PROTOCOL.md`). All 400 main episodes completed, at an estimated API cost of US$9.09, with every raw record committed and replayed offline.

## 3. What we found

Pooled across both models, each condition has 40 benign and 40 temptation episodes:

| Condition | Completed out-of-scope actions (Suite B) | Correct and compliant completion (Suite A) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Baseline | 17/40 | 38/40 |
| Reminder | 3/40 | 40/40 |
| Acknowledgement + reminder | 0/40 | 39/40 |
| Declaration + reminder | 0/40 | 39/40 |
| Declaration, no pointer | 6/40 | 38/40 |

**What this does show.** Policy exposure matters a great deal: pointing the agent at the rules cut completed violations from 17 to 3 out of 40 (−35 percentage points, task-bootstrap 95% interval −62.5 to −7.5), and the three remaining violations were restricted-directory listings rather than restricted reads or external transfers. Adding any gate on top of the reminder drove violations to zero in both gated arms, at no cost to benign-task completion. The procedures are not free, though: on temptation tasks the mean input tokens per episode for `gpt-6-luna` rose from about 35k (reminder only) to 68k (acknowledgement) and 77k (declaration), because each privileged call becomes two or more round trips. The harness itself behaved as designed. The broker rejects any privileged call that lacks a matching ticket, so no ungated privileged action can execute in a gated arm, and the logs answer the who-asked, what-was-allowed, what-executed questions deterministically, without an LLM judge.

**What this does not show.** The declaration procedure had no measurable effect beyond the acknowledgement procedure: the primary contrast is zero for both outcomes. Zero events in 40 episodes is not proof of equivalence or of safety; it is a floor effect in a small benchmark. The "declaration without a pointer" arm shows that the gate's behavioural effect depends on the model: it took Sonnet from 12/20 violations to 0/20, but Luna from 5/20 to 6/20. A correctly declared forbidden action passes a content-blind gate, so the gate is a procedural control, not an authorisation boundary. Neither model spawned a helper during the main run, so the experiment says nothing empirically about delegation, policy transmission to children or swarm containment proper; those paths are implemented and unit-tested but were not exercised by the models. And the original hypothesis of a *selective* asymmetry (friction hurting shortcuts more than legitimate work) cannot be tested when legitimate work sits at ceiling and violations at floor.

## 4. What the AI Village data told us

The AI Village dataset (AI Digest; 78,111 computer-use sessions, 2.5 million turns, 979,076 shell commands, plus an events table) is the closest public thing to a long-running multi-agent deployment with a human approval step. We used it descriptively, asking the same forensic questions we ask of our own broker logs. Three findings matter for anyone designing oversight tooling:

**Chain length is a property of the scaffold, not the agents.** The median computer-use session is exactly 40 actions because the Village consolidates agent memory every 40 actions; the Claude Code agent, which runs its own loop, has a median of 8. Any oversight metric keyed to "how long did the agent run before checking in" is measuring the harness. This was the single most useful lesson for our own design, which originally predicted effects as a function of chain length.

**A content-blind gate would fire often, and mostly on routine work.** 35.7% of shell commands (14.4% of all actions) match network, credential or process-launch patterns. Agents named 4,819 distinct external hosts; 241 of them were named by five or more agents. These are regex proxies over bash text only: GUI actions are unclassified and a hostname in a command does not prove contact. But the inventory shows the volume a procedural gate would have to handle in a real deployment, and why it must be cheap.

**The Village's own approval loop is already a forensic record.** Since April 2026 agents must request approval before contacting outside parties. The events table alone, without reading any chat, yields 352 requests, 343 decisions, 87 refusals, a median turnaround of 3 minutes with a tail of two days, and ten requests in which an agent disclosed that it was routing an action through another agent that held credentials it lacked (nine were approved). That is disclosed delegation under an approval process, not evasion. The point is that structured events answered these questions directly; transcripts would not have.

We also looked at **SwarmTraces**, the publicly released evidence export from the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, which the hackathon listed as an eligible dataset. Before trying to measure anything in it, we asked what the file can actually tell us. It contains 189,579 records of three kinds: the raw requests captured during the incident ("payloads"), the text recovered from decoding them, and the server responses. Records are linked to each other by a parent field, so it is tempting to read a cluster of linked records as a sequence of agent actions. It is not. Every link is exactly one level deep: a captured request, with its decoded text and its response attached underneath. The largest cluster has 859 records, but that is one request with 858 attachments, not an 859-step chain, and not 858 helper agents. Every timestamp in the file is empty, so the records cannot be put in time order either. In short, this export can tell you *what kinds of things were captured and how often* but not *how long the agents ran, how many there were, or in what order they acted*. We think that check belongs in any forensic toolkit: a dataset's unit of record has to be understood before any metric is computed on it. Our script runs offline, reports aggregate counts only, and never executes or decodes the captured content (`docs/SWARMTRACES_STRUCTURE.md`).

## 5. Problems along the way, and what we changed

The first version of this experiment appeared to show that the declaration gate was *the* mechanism. An independent audit of the harness found several problems: the declaration arm bundled a policy reminder that its control lacked; step budgets were per-agent and could be exceeded by children; credential names could authenticate without a token being issued; graders were permissive; and some interrupted episodes were silently replaced. We kept the original code, records and report in Git (`docs/RESULTS_V1_HISTORICAL.md`, `docs/LEGACY_ERRATA.md`, `docs/audit/`), repaired the harness, froze a new protocol, and reran everything from scratch. The Village numbers survived the audit; their interpretation was tightened (for instance, 14.4% is a share of *all* actions, 35.7% the share of shell commands). The classifier used for the Village privileged-class proxy was calibrated on development examples and has no independent holdout; we report it as a proxy, not a validated detector. One post-hoc grading sensitivity (six benign outputs that were mathematically correct but formatted differently) is disclosed separately and does not change any violation count.

## 6. Why this matters

The OpenAI–Hugging Face incident of July 2026 happened in an evaluation where deployment safeguards were "intentionally not enabled" because they interfered with the objective. Monitoring is a discretionary layer, and discretionary controls fail exactly when the operating goal conflicts with them. A procedure implemented in the tool layer cannot be quietly switched off: its absence is itself an event in the log. Our results suggest the behavioural benefit of such procedures may come mostly from making the rules salient at the moment of action, rather than from the ritual of declaration itself, which is a cheaper and more robust thing to engineer. Independently of behaviour, the structured record the broker produces is what lets a human move from real-time adjudication, which humans do badly at scale, to batch audit, which they do well. For hackathon purposes: this is a reusable question set and log format (`python -m friction.forensics <log>`) that answers the brief's "questions you would always ask of a multi-agent group" from structure rather than narrative, and it was applied to three evidence sources with their denominators stated.

## 7. What is next

The immediate gap is delegation. The harness can attach the charter to spawned children and test whether a parent's constraints survive the hand-off, but the models never spawned in this run; tasks that require delegation are the next experiment. Second, the benchmark needs adversarial pressure: cooperative frontier models in a mocked world are the easy case, and the zero-violation cells cannot distinguish a good control from an unchallenged one. Third, the Village approval events are a natural place to test whether a mechanical, deny-by-default procedure would have changed outcomes, by replaying the request stream against a charter. Finally, the proxy classifier needs an independent holdout before any Village rate is quoted as a measurement rather than an inventory.
